How much to charge for dog walking: a simple way to set your rates

Skip the guesswork. Start from your own time and costs, then check the result against what's normal where you live.

There is no single "right" price for a dog walk. What owners pay varies a lot by area: city or suburb, how busy local walkers are, and what's included. So instead of copying a number from the internet, use a method that works anywhere: work out the lowest price that's worth your time, then see where you fit locally.

1

Decide what an hour of your time is worth

Pick the amount you want to earn for every hour you work, before costs and tax. Think about what you could earn in a regular job nearby, and add something for the fact that you're self-employed: no paid holidays, no sick pay, and quiet weeks.

2

Count the real time each visit takes

A "30-minute walk" is never 30 minutes. Add up everything:

  • Driving or walking to the home, and on to the next one
  • Getting in, the lead on, water and towel-off afterwards
  • The walk itself
  • A quick update or photo for the owner

Time a few real visits. The total is the time you're actually selling.

3

Add your costs per visit

Fuel or transport, waste bags, treats, wear on leads and gear, your insurance, phone and app fees, card fees. Add up a typical month and divide by the number of visits. Track it for a couple of months and the number gets more accurate.

4

Put it together

Lowest sensible price = (real time per visit × your hourly figure) + costs per visit. Remember you'll also owe tax on what's left, so don't treat everything as take-home pay.

Use your own numbers. In your area this might be well above or below what others charge, which is what step 5 is for.

5

Check it against your area

Look at what local walkers and pet sitters list on their websites, flyers and local listings, and ask around (vets, groomers, other owners). Note what's included: walk length, solo or group, photo updates, keys. Then decide where you sit:

  • If your lowest price is above the local norm: cut travel time by grouping clients by area, offer group walks, or focus on clients who value something extra (solo walks, puppies, reliability).
  • If it's below: you don't need to be the cheapest. Price near the middle and compete on being reliable and easy to deal with.

Pricing the extras

Write these down up front so you're not deciding on the spot:

  • Extra dog from the same home: usually a smaller add-on, not a second full price.
  • Longer walks: price the extra time with the same method, not just double.
  • Puppy visits and medication: these often take more care and attention, so they can carry a premium.
  • Holidays and last-minute bookings: many walkers add a surcharge. Say so in advance.
  • Overnight or day-long sits: count all the hours you're committed, not only the active ones.
  • Pet taxi or vet runs: time plus distance.

Packages and regulars

Weekly regulars are the backbone of a walking business: predictable income and less admin. A small discount for a fixed weekly schedule or a prepaid bundle of walks can be worth it, as long as the discounted price is still above your lowest sensible price. Keep track of how many prepaid walks each owner has left.

When to raise your prices

  • You're fully booked and turning people away.
  • Your costs per visit have gone up.
  • It's been about a year since you last reviewed them.

Give existing clients plenty of notice, a short honest reason, and the new price in writing. New clients can start on the new price straight away.

Not financial or tax advice. This is a general method for thinking about prices. What you owe in tax and any licenses you need depend on where you live.
