Cleaning business expense list: what to track

A plain checklist of the costs that come with running a cleaning business on your own, and a simple habit that keeps them in order.

Most cleaners know roughly what they earn. Far fewer know what they actually keep. The gap is expenses: the bottles, the fuel, the insurance renewal you forgot was coming. Tracking them properly does three things for you:

  • You see your real profit, not just money in.
  • You can tell whether your prices still cover your costs.
  • When it's time to sort out your taxes, the numbers and receipts are already there.
Not tax advice. Which costs you can claim, and how, depends on where you live and how your business is set up. This page is about tracking. Check what's deductible with your local tax office or an accountant.

The expense checklist

These are the categories most one-person cleaning businesses run into. You won't have all of them, and you may have a few of your own.

Cleaning suppliesSprays, detergents, disinfectant, cloths, sponges, mop heads, gloves, bin bags, paper towels.
EquipmentVacuums, mops and buckets, steam cleaners, extension poles, caddies, step stools, repairs to any of these.
Vehicle & fuelFuel, parking, tolls, servicing and repairs. Many tax systems let you claim either a mileage rate or your actual vehicle costs, not both, so check which applies to you. See below.
InsuranceLiability cover, any bonding, equipment or vehicle cover used for work.
MarketingFlyers, business cards, a website, online listings, paid ads, referral thank-yous.
Phone & softwareThe work share of your phone bill, scheduling or invoicing apps, cloud storage.
Subcontractors / helpersAnyone you pay to help on a job, even occasionally.
UniformsBranded shirts, aprons, shoe covers, work clothing used only for the job.

OtherAnything that doesn't fit. If "Other" keeps growing, give it its own category.

The easy-to-miss ones

  • Small top-ups. A bottle of spray grabbed on the way to a job. These add up quickly and are the first receipts to get lost.
  • Yearly bills. Insurance, licenses and software subscriptions that renew once a year. Note the renewal date so it doesn't surprise you.
  • Things that wear out. Vacuum filters, mop heads and microfibre cloths are real costs even if each one is small.
  • Client-specific costs. If a client asks for a particular product, log it. It helps you price that client properly.

Mileage: track the trips, not just the fuel

Driving between clients is often one of the bigger costs of a cleaning business, and it is easy to underestimate. Some places let you use a standard rate per mile or kilometre, others ask for actual costs. Either way, a simple trip log is useful:

  • Date
  • From and to (or the client's name)
  • Distance
  • Purpose ("Rivera household, standard clean")

Log trips on the day. Rebuilding a year of driving from memory in spring is miserable and unreliable.

Receipts: a system that takes no willpower

  1. Snap it at the till. Take a photo of every receipt before it goes in your pocket.
  2. One folder. Keep the photos in one album or cloud folder per year, for example "Receipts 2026".
  3. Name or note it. Date, shop and amount is enough: 2026-03-04 hardware store 23.40.
  4. Mark it as saved. When you log the expense, mark that the receipt is saved, so you can see at a glance which ones are missing.

How long you need to keep receipts varies by country, often several years. Check the rule where you live.

The 10-minute weekly habit

Pick one fixed time each week, such as Friday after your last job, and do this:

  1. Log the week's expenses from your receipt photos.
  2. Log the week's trips if you didn't do it on the day.
  3. Check which jobs are still unpaid and send reminders.
  4. Glance at the month so far: money in, money out, what's left.

Ten minutes a week is far easier than a lost weekend at tax time, and you'll spot problems (a client who always pays late, supplies costing more than you thought) while you can still do something about them.

Is my pricing covering my costs?

Once you've tracked a couple of months, add up your expenses and divide by the number of jobs you did. That's roughly what each job costs you before your own time. If that number has crept up, your prices may need to follow. What clients pay for a clean varies a lot by area, so compare with what's normal locally, not with figures from somewhere else.
